## What are the main types of tiles?

**The main types of tiles for houses are clay (terracotta, Multani and khaprail), ceramic and porcelain, natural stone such as granite and marble, and cement or concrete tiles. Each differs in porosity, hardness, maintenance and look, so the right pick depends on the room, how much water it sees, and whether it is indoors or outdoors.**

## What are clay and terracotta tiles?

Clay tiles are shaped from natural clay and fired in a kiln. Terracotta is the familiar warm red-brown version, and in Pakistan you will hear Multani tiles, otti and gutka used for related clay products. Fired clay is usually porous, which gives it a soft, earthy look and a surface that many people find comfortable underfoot. The trade-off is that unsealed clay can absorb water and stains, so ask your fixer about a breathable sealer and a sloped, well-drained base outdoors. Colour varies a little from piece to piece, which is part of the appeal and also a reason to order the full quantity from one batch.

Clay suits verandas, courtyards, living areas, terraces and some feature walls. Our [12x12 inch terracotta tile](/products/12x12-inch-terracotta-tiles) is a common starting point for floors. Roofs use a different clay product altogether: [khaprail roof tiles](/products/spanish-natural-red-khaprail-tiles), which are profiled to shed rain.

## What are ceramic and porcelain tiles?

Ceramic tiles are made from clay and minerals, shaped and fired, then usually coated with a glaze. The glaze is a glassy layer that makes the surface easier to wipe and gives the tile its colour and pattern. Porcelain is generally made from a finer mix fired hotter, and is usually described as denser and less absorbent than standard ceramic. Exact water absorption and strength vary by product, so look for the maker's data on the carton rather than relying on the word "porcelain" alone.

These tiles are popular for kitchens, bathrooms and indoor floors because glazed surfaces are easy to clean. ## What are natural stone tiles?

Stone tiles are cut from quarried rock. Granite is generally hard and dense, marble is known for veining and a polished look, and sandstone and similar stones give a rougher, natural face. Stone is often used for wall cladding, facades, steps and feature areas. It can be heavy, so check that the wall or floor suits the weight, and some stones need sealing and more careful cleaning than glazed tiles. Because each stone is a natural product, patterns and shades differ between pieces. ## What are cement and concrete tiles?

Cement tiles, sometimes called concrete tiles, are made by pressing or casting a cement-based mix. They are common for pavers, jali blocks, outdoor floor pieces and patterned surfaces. Many are designed for outdoor use and can carry vehicles or foot traffic depending on thickness and design, so ask for the maker's guidance for your use. Cement is also porous and may need sealing. ## Which tile type suits which room?

There is no single best tile, only a better match for each space. As general guidance:

- **Living room and bedrooms:** large clay squares, porcelain or polished stone give a calm, continuous floor.
- **Kitchen floor:** glazed ceramic or porcelain for easy cleaning; sealed terracotta if you like the warm look and accept some upkeep.
- **Kitchen wall and backsplash:** glazed ceramic mosaics and other glazed tiles that wipe clean.
- **Bathroom:** glazed ceramic or porcelain; check the slip resistance information for any floor tile.
- **Terrace, porch and courtyard:** terracotta, otti, pavers or stone with a textured face, laid to drain.
- **Front wall and boundary:** stone cladding, brick-style clay, or jali for ventilation and shade.
- **Roof:** khaprail clay tiles on a suitable frame.

## How do you compare tile types before buying?

Start with five questions. Is the tile indoors or outdoors? Will water sit on it? How much cleaning are you willing to do? Does the surface need grip? And what is the weight and thickness for your wall or floor? Then compare the same details across materials. Glazed tiles usually ask for the least maintenance, clay and cement ask for a sealer and a little care, and stone asks for care that depends on the type. None of this is a fixed rule, so treat it as a starting checklist and ask each supplier for written specifications.

## What should you check on any tile?

Look at the size, thickness, finish and batch. Ask for a physical sample and view it in daylight, dry and wet. Confirm whether the shade varies between pieces. Measure the area and add a margin for cuts and breakage; diagonal layouts and rooms with many corners need more. Ask your fixer which adhesive and grout suit the material. Finally, keep the quote showing the tile name, unit, quantity and date, so that you can compare suppliers fairly.
