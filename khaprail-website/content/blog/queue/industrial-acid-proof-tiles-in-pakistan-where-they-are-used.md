## What are acid proof tiles?

**Acid proof tiles are dense, low-porosity tiles or bricks, usually fired ceramic or clay, laid with chemical-resistant jointing so that floors and walls can withstand contact with acids and other corrosive liquids in industrial settings.** The tile alone is not enough; the jointing material and the layer beneath it decide how well the whole surface performs.

## Where are acid proof tiles typically used?

They tend to appear wherever acids, alkalis or cleaning chemicals touch the surface regularly. Common examples include:

- chemical and fertiliser plants
- battery manufacturing and charging areas
- textile dyeing and processing units
- pharmaceutical and laboratory floors
- food and beverage plants with frequent chemical washdowns
- effluent and neutralisation tanks and drain channels
- metal pickling and plating shops

The exact need depends on the chemical, its concentration, temperature and how long it stays on the surface. A floor that sees an occasional splash is a different problem from a tank that holds acid all day.

## What makes a tile acid proof?

Two things matter most: low water absorption and a dense body. A tile that absorbs liquid can pull the chemical inside and be damaged from within. Dense, well-fired material resists this better. Some designs are made as thick tiles or bricks to cope with heavy loads and repeated cleaning. However, resistance is not universal. A material that copes with one acid may be damaged by another, or by strong alkalis, at a certain temperature. Treat any broad claim such as "resistant to all chemicals" with caution and ask for the supplier's test data for your specific chemical.

## Why do the joints matter as much as the tile?

In an acid-exposed floor, the grout line is often the weak point. Ordinary cement grout can be attacked by acids and may break down, letting the chemical reach the layer underneath. Industrial installations typically use chemical-resistant mortars or resin-based jointing designed for the exposure, and may add a protective membrane below the tile. The type depends on the chemical and the site. Ask your engineer or installer to specify the whole system, not only the tile.

## Do acid proof tiles need special floors and slopes?

Often yes. Floors in chemical areas are usually sloped towards drains so spills run off instead of pooling, and the sub-base must be strong and properly waterproofed. Plan the slope, the drains, the skirting and the wall-floor junction before you buy tiles. A well-chosen tile on a poorly prepared floor can still fail.

## Are acid proof tiles slippery?

They can be, especially when wet or oily. In industrial settings, safety rules matter, so ask about surface texture and slip resistance for areas where staff walk. A textured finish usually gives better grip than a glossy one, but may be harder to clean. Your safety officer should weigh in before you decide.

## How do acid proof tiles compare with ordinary floor tiles?

Ordinary ceramic or terracotta floor tiles are made for homes and offices, where the main risks are foot traffic, water and cleaning products. They are not designed for regular acid exposure. Acid proof tiles are denser and are installed with specialised jointing, so they typically cost more and take more skill to lay. If your floor will only see domestic cleaning, you probably do not need them. If your floor handles corrosive liquids, an ordinary tile is not a safe substitute. ## What should you ask before you buy?

Write down the chemicals, their concentration and temperature, and the type of load or traffic. Ask the supplier for the tile's water absorption and any chemical resistance information in writing. Ask which jointing mortar they recommend and whether the whole quantity comes from one batch. Ask about thickness, size and surface texture. Finally, have a qualified engineer confirm that the system fits your process. We are a clay tile maker, not an industrial lining contractor, so we cannot certify a tile for your chemicals.

## How do you look after an acid proof floor?

Maintenance keeps the system working. Rinse spills promptly instead of leaving them to dwell, because contact time matters. Use cleaning agents that your supplier says are compatible, and avoid dragging heavy sharp-edged equipment across the surface. Inspect the joints regularly, since a cracked or eroded joint is the first sign that a chemical may be reaching the layer below. Repair joints early with the same jointing material, and keep a record of what was used. In hot, humid plants such as those in Karachi or Faisalabad, check drains and wall-floor junctions more often, as standing water and heat can speed up wear. Training staff to report damage quickly usually costs far less than relaying a whole floor.
