## Spanish, French or flat khaprail tiles: which profile suits your roof?

Ask three roofers in Lahore about khaprail profiles and you may hear three slightly different answers. Spanish, French and flat are trade names for tile families, and the names tell you the style more than an exact dimension. That is fine, as long as you know what to ask. This guide explains what each family generally looks like, how the choice affects the look and the laying of your roof, and which questions to put to your supplier before you order. It uses the profiles Pak Clay lists in its catalogue and avoids claims we cannot back with a spec sheet.

---

## What are khaprail tile profiles?

**A khaprail tile profile is the shape of the tile as seen from above and in cross-section: curved and wavy, flat with interlocking edges, or flat with a rounded or rectangular end. The profile affects the look of the roof, how tiles overlap and how water runs off.**

Khaprail tiles are fired clay roof tiles laid in overlapping rows on a sloped roof. They shed rain by shape and overlap, not by sealant, so the profile and the way it is laid matter together. For the laying side, see our guide to [how khaprail roof tiles are installed](/blog/how-khaprail-roof-tiles-are-installed-slope-battens-and-overlap).

## What are Spanish khaprail tiles?

Spanish-style clay tiles are generally the ones with a pronounced curve, giving the roof a rolling, wave-like texture with strong shadow lines. The look is associated with Mediterranean houses and with many traditional-style villas, and in Pakistan it is a popular choice where owners want a classic terracotta roof.

Pak Clay lists [Spanish natural red khaprail tiles](/products/spanish-natural-red-khaprail-tiles). Natural red means unglazed clay with colour running through the body, so it tends to weather gracefully and blend with brick and stone. Because curved tiles have more relief, they can hold dust and leaves in the troughs, so a roof with trees nearby benefits from regular checks.

## What are French khaprail tiles?

French-style tiles are generally flatter and more regular in outline than the Spanish curve, with a neater, more uniform look across the roof. The effect is cleaner and more formal, which suits contemporary and colonial-style elevations.

The catalogue lists [French natural red](/products/french-natural-red-khaprail-tiles) and [French green glazed](/products/french-green-glazed-khaprail-tiles). The glazed version has a fired-on coating, giving a deep green that does not rely on the clay colour. It is easier to wipe clean and stands out, so check a real sample against your wall colour and the surroundings, since green on a roof reads differently from green on a screen.

## What are flat khaprail tiles?

Flat tiles lie close to the roof plane with little or no wave. Pak Clay lists two flat shapes, [flat half circle](/products/flat-half-circle-khaprail-tiles) and [flat rectangular](/products/flat-rectangular-khaprail-tiles). Half-circle ends give a scalloped, fish-scale pattern when laid in staggered rows, while rectangular tiles give a straighter, more modern grid.

Flat profiles have a quiet, even look, and they suit simple rooflines. Because they show less relief, the laying lines, overlap and ridge finish are very visible, which makes workmanship more noticeable. We would not describe any profile as automatically better at keeping water out; drainage depends on slope, overlap, underlay and detailing.

## How do the three profiles compare?

| | Spanish | French | Flat |
|---|---|---|---|
| General look | Curved, wavy, strong shadows | Neat, even, formal | Quiet, scalloped or gridded |
| Catalogue examples | Natural red | Natural red, green glazed | Half circle, rectangular |
| Best for | Traditional and Mediterranean style | Colonial or contemporary style | Simple rooflines, modern look |
| Watch out for | Debris in troughs | Colour choice with glaze | Visible workmanship |

This is a style comparison, not a performance ranking. Weight, size, coverage per square foot and minimum slope differ by product, so ask Pak Clay for those details for the exact tile on your shortlist and share them with your engineer.

## Which profile suits your roof best?

Work through these questions:

1. **What is the house style?** Match the roof to the architecture so it looks intended, not added on.
2. **What is the roof shape?** Complex roofs with hips and valleys need more cutting and detailing, whichever profile you choose.
3. **What slope do you have?** Different profiles can ask for different minimum slopes, so confirm with your supplier and engineer rather than guessing.
4. **What maintenance can you accept?** Curved tiles may collect debris; glazed tiles clean easily but show scratches.
5. **What is the budget?** Prices depend on profile, size, finish, quantity and transport, so compare dated written quotes.

If heat is your main worry, finish colour and ventilation under the roof matter as well, and our [roof tiles for heat guide](/blog/best-roof-tiles-for-heat-in-pakistan-keeping-homes-cooler) discusses them in general terms.

## Should you mix profiles on one house?

Some owners use one profile on the main roof and another on a porch or a boundary-wall cap. It can look deliberate if the colours match and the lines are kept simple, but different profiles can ask for different fixing and ridge details, so tell your contractor early. Ordering every profile from one batch, with a few spares, also keeps colour consistent for future repairs.

## How many tiles will you need?

Each profile covers a different area per tile once overlaps are accounted for, so the tile count changes with the profile. Our [tile calculation guide](/blog/how-to-calculate-how-many-khaprail-tiles-your-roof-needs) walks through the method, including an allowance for breakage and cuts. Ask your supplier for the coverage figure for the specific tile; do not borrow a number from another profile.
