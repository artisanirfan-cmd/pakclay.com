## How are khaprail roof tiles installed in Pakistan, and what should you check?

If you are planning a khaprail roof in Lahore or any other Pakistani city, the tile is only half of the job. A good clay tile laid on a poor frame will still leak, and an average tile laid on a well-built frame can serve for years. This guide explains how khaprail roof tiles are installed in general terms: the slope, the battens, the overlap, the fixing and the finishing at ridges and edges. It is written for homeowners who want to ask their contractor sensible questions, not as a site manual. Your structural engineer and your tile supplier have the final say on your roof.

---

## What are khaprail tiles?

**Khaprail tiles are fired clay roofing tiles, shaped from natural clay and baked in a kiln until hard. They are laid in overlapping rows on a sloped roof so that water runs from one tile onto the next and off the roof.** The profile can be curved, flat or half-round, and the finish can be natural red or glazed.

Because the tiles shed water by overlapping rather than by being sealed, correct laying matters more than almost any other factor.

## Why does the roof slope matter for khaprail tiles?

A tiled roof works by gravity. Water must run down the surface faster than it can find a way under the tile, so the slope (pitch) of the roof is the first thing to settle. Steep roofs shed water quickly, and very shallow roofs are more likely to let wind-driven rain creep under the laps.

Different profiles are designed for different minimum slopes, and the figure depends on the tile shape and the length of the overlap. We do not publish one number for every profile here, because a wrong figure could cause leaks. Instead, ask your supplier for the minimum slope for the exact profile you chose, and ask your engineer to confirm that your roof frame meets it. If your house has a flat concrete roof, a sloped frame or a built-up slope may be needed before tiling.

## What is the structure under the tiles?

Under the tiles there are usually several layers, and the exact build depends on your roof:

- **The structure.** This can be a timber or steel frame, or a concrete slab with a sloped build-up. It must carry the weight of the tiles, so ask an engineer to check the load.
- **An underlay or waterproofing layer.** Many tiled roofs rely on a secondary layer under the tiles as a second line of defence against wind-blown rain. Ask your contractor what they plan to use.
- **Battens.** These are thin strips fixed across the slope, and the tiles hang or sit on them.
- **Ventilation.** A gap under the tiles lets warm air move out, and it also helps the roof dry after rain.

## What are battens and how far apart should they be?

Battens are the rails that hold the tile rows at the right spacing. The gap between them is set by the tile length and the overlap you want, so it is not a fixed number across all tiles. The usual method is simple: lay out a few tiles on the ground in the overlapped position, measure the distance from one row to the next, and mark that spacing on the roof. A consistent batten gauge keeps the courses straight and the overlap even.

Ask your contractor to set out the battens from a real sample tile, not from a guess. If the tiles in your batch vary slightly in length, a little measurement at the start saves a lot of adjustment later. Treated timber or corrosion-resistant fixings are generally preferred, because rust or rot in the battens shortens the life of the whole roof.

## How much should khaprail tiles overlap?

The overlap is the length by which each row covers the row below it. A larger overlap generally gives better protection against rain and wind, and it also uses more tiles per square foot of roof. A very small overlap saves tiles and increases the risk of leaks. The right overlap depends on your profile and your roof slope: a lower slope usually asks for more overlap.

## How are the tiles fixed down?

Fixing depends on the profile, the slope and the local wind. In general, tiles may be held by their own weight and hooks on the battens, by nails or clips on some or all rows, or by mortar bedding at ridges and edges. Roofs in windy or exposed places usually need more fixing than sheltered ones. Ask your contractor which tiles will be fixed, how, and why. A vague answer is a warning sign.

## How are ridges, edges and valleys finished?

The ridge is the top line of the roof, and it is usually capped with special ridge pieces or tiles bedded and fixed so water cannot get in. The edges (verges and eaves) and the valleys, where two slopes meet, are where most leaks start, because water is concentrated there. Ask to see how the contractor will finish these areas, and whether the work includes flashing at walls and chimneys. Good detail work at these points is more important than a perfect field of plain tiles.

## What should you prepare before the tiles arrive?

- **Confirm the frame** and slope with your engineer before ordering.
- **Order a sample** and keep it, so the contractor can set out battens from it.
- **Order extra tiles** for breakage and future repairs. Colour can vary slightly between batches, so keep a few spares.
- **Store tiles carefully** off the ground, stacked as the supplier advises.
- **Plan safe access**, because clay tiles can crack if walked on carelessly.
