## Which tiles work best for modern bathrooms and kitchens?

Bathrooms and kitchens are the two rooms in a house where tiles work hardest. They meet water, steam, oil and daily cleaning, and they are also the rooms people look at most when they judge a renovation. If you are searching for modern bathroom tiles, washroom tiles or a kitchen backsplash, this guide covers the materials that tend to hold up, the layouts that look current, and the practical checks that matter more than the catalogue photo. ## What makes a bathroom or kitchen tile "modern"?

**Modern bathroom and kitchen tiles are usually glazed ceramic or porcelain pieces in clean shapes, such as subway rectangles, hexagons and small mosaics, in calm colours, with tight, even grout lines. The look is simple, but the tile must also suit wet use: easy to wipe, low in water absorption, and with grip on any floor surface.**

## Which materials suit wet rooms?

Glazed ceramic and porcelain are the usual choices for walls and floors in wet rooms, because the glaze makes the surface easy to wipe and the body is typically less absorbent than open clay. Ceramic mosaics are small glazed pieces, often supplied on sheets, and they work well on curved or small surfaces. Natural stone can be used, but may need sealing and more careful cleaning. Terracotta is a warm option for kitchens and some bathroom floors, but it is porous, so it needs a sealer and care. ## What are good kitchen and backsplash tile ideas?

A backsplash is the strip of wall between the counter and the cabinets, and it takes splashes from the stove and sink. Glazed tiles are a sensible choice because you can wipe oil off the surface. Popular ideas include subway rectangles in a brick pattern, small kite or diamond shapes, and a single bright accent colour against neutral cabinets. The catalogue lists a [yellow glazed subway kitchen wall tile](/products/yellow-glazed-ceramic-mosaic-subway-kitchen-wall-tiles) and a [light green glazed kite kitchen wall tile](/products/light-green-glazed-kite-ceramic-mosaic-kitchen-wall-tiles) as examples. Keep grout lines narrow behind the stove, and ask your fixer about a grout that resists stains. If you are asking which marble to put in the kitchen, remember that polished marble can mark from acids and oil, so many households prefer glazed tiles on the wall and a harder, easier-care surface on the counter.

## Which colours and finishes work best?

Light colours make a small washroom feel larger, while deeper greens and blues give a bathroom a calm, spa-like feel. Glossy finishes reflect light and wipe clean, which suits walls. Matt finishes hide water marks and generally offer more grip, which suits floors, though you should still check the maker's slip resistance information for the product. Mixing one glossy wall tile with a matt floor tile is a common approach. Whatever you pick, view the sample in the room's own light and compare it with your fixtures before ordering.

## How do you handle floors versus walls?

Treat them as two separate decisions. Wall tiles can be thin and glossy because nobody walks on them. Floor tiles need grip when wet, enough strength for the base, and a slope towards the drain in a wet area. Never assume a wall tile is suitable for a floor; check the product's stated use. In kitchens, a floor tile also has to cope with dropped utensils, so ask about thickness and hardness, and about the recommended adhesive for the tile and base.

## How much tile do you need, and what should you ask?

Measure the wall and floor area separately, subtract doors and windows, and add a margin for cuts and breakage. Small mosaics and diagonal layouts waste more. Ask for a sample, confirm the size and thickness in writing, and ask whether the full quantity comes from one batch, since shade can vary between firings. Request a quote that shows the unit, the quantity and the date. ## What mistakes should you avoid?

The common ones are choosing by photo alone, skipping a sample, mixing batches, ignoring the slope to the drain, and using an unsealed porous tile in a wet spot. Another is crowding a small room with several patterns; one feature is usually enough. A last one is forgetting maintenance. Glazed tiles are easy, but grout still needs cleaning, and any natural material asks for a sealer and gentle products. Ask your fixer, and ask us, if you are unsure.
