## Are terracotta kitchen wall tiles a good idea for a Pakistani kitchen?

A Pakistani kitchen works hard. Oil spatters from tarka and frying, steam rises from the pot, and the wall behind the stove takes the heat. Terracotta kitchen wall tiles bring a warm, earthy look, but fired clay is porous, so it needs more thought than a glossy glazed tile. This guide explains how terracotta behaves behind a hob and a sink, where it works best, where a glazed alternative may be wiser, and what to ask before you order. ## Can terracotta handle heat behind a stove?

Clay is fired at high temperature in manufacture, so ordinary cooking heat on a wall is generally not a problem for the tile body itself. The weak points are usually the fixing materials and the joints, not the clay. Ask your tile fixer to use an adhesive and grout suited to a kitchen, and to leave the manufacturer's recommended gap near open flames. Keep in mind that a wall right beside a gas burner collects grease quickly, so plan for easy cleaning in that zone. A tile that survives heat but stains from oil is only half a success.

## Will oil and splashes stain terracotta?

They can, because natural terracotta is porous. Unsealed clay may absorb oil, turmeric, tomato and water, leaving darker patches that are hard to remove. This is the main trade-off. There are three common ways to manage it:

- **Use a sealer.** Many fixers apply a breathable or penetrating sealer on natural clay in a kitchen. Ask which product suits your tile and how often to reapply it, because the answer varies by product.
- **Limit natural terracotta to the dry zones.** Keep it on the wall beside the window, the dining side or an accent panel, and use a glazed tile directly behind the hob.
- **Choose a glazed tile for the splash zone.** A glazed surface is sealed by its glaze and usually wipes clean more easily.

There is no single right answer. It depends on how much you cook, how often you clean and how much patina you can enjoy.

## Where does terracotta work best in a kitchen?

Terracotta tends to suit a kitchen where you want a warm, rustic or traditional feel. Good places include the wall between counter and cabinets away from the stove, an arch or niche, a breakfast counter front and the wall of an open kitchen that faces the dining area. In a small kitchen, a lighter shade and small tiles can keep the room from feeling heavy. In a large kitchen, a bigger tile with fewer joints is easier to wipe down. Look at the tile in the actual light of your kitchen, since clay shades change between morning and evening.

## Which shape and size should you choose?

Small tiles create more grout lines, which add pattern but also collect grease. Larger tiles reduce the number of joints and are quicker to clean. ## How should terracotta kitchen tiles be installed?

Installation matters as much as the tile. Walls should be dry, level and clean before fixing. Clay tiles are often soaked briefly or dampened before fixing in some methods, so ask your fixer what suits the tile and adhesive. Use grout that is suitable for kitchens and clean the surface as you go, because grout haze is hard to remove from a porous tile. Allow adhesive and grout to cure for the time the product label gives before you seal the surface and start cooking against it. Leave a clear gap or use a heat-suitable material directly beside a flame.

## How do you clean and care for terracotta in the kitchen?

Wipe splashes quickly with a soft damp cloth. For routine cleaning, a mild soap and water usually works. Avoid strong acids or harsh bleach on unsealed clay, since they can mark or weaken the surface; test any cleaner on a spare tile first. Reapply the sealer when water no longer beads on the surface or as the product instructions advise. A little ageing is part of the charm of terracotta, but tell your household what to expect before the first curry splash.
